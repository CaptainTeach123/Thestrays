# Voice & Prose Style — *The Strays*

Derived by close-reading the Chapter 1 rough draft ("The Move"). This file
captures **how the author actually writes**, so future chapters sound like the
same hand wrote them. `style-guide.md` sets the mechanical rules (first-person,
past tense); this file sets the *texture*. Where they overlap, both apply.

---

## One-line voice statement

A self-deprecating, wry, digressive first-person narrator telling a horror story
in the voice of a stand-up-comic loser — funny and conversational in the mundane,
then falling quiet and earnest the moment the uncanny arrives.

**The whole style is that tonal contrast.** The comedy is not decoration; it is
the thing the horror ruptures. If a chapter is all jokes, it isn't this book. If
it's all dread, it isn't either.

---

## Core traits (with examples from the sample)

1. **Retrospective narrator who foreshadows.** He's telling this after the fact
   and lets doom leak backward into calm moments.
   > "This newfound optimism would come to a quick and unceremonious end as we
   > entered the outskirts of our new community."
   > "Looking back, it seemed so logical to blame the meds rather than accept that
   > maybe something else was going on."
   Use this sparingly to prime dread — a single forward-looking sentence before a
   scene turns.

2. **Self-deprecating comedy about his own failure.** His lowest material is his
   own life.
   > "a promising writer with, as the Times put it, 'unlimited potential'. Too
   > many failed projects later, I had excelled at exactly one thing: proving my
   > early supporters wrong."
   > "(if something still qualifies as a novel if no one reads it)"

3. **Parenthetical & em-dash asides.** He interrupts himself to editorialize.
   > "a public—sometimes too public—admission"
   > "a candle shop that seemed to specialize in weak—uh, subtle—smells"
   These self-corrections ("weak—uh, subtle—") are a signature move.

4. **Comic hyperbole & mock-grandiosity.** Ordinary things get inflated for laughs.
   > "the world's fastest shower. Rebecca might have a different opinion of the
   > speeds of my suds but she would be mistaken."
   > "employer-subsidized libations"
   > "the Crabcake Factory—a name so similar to a certain other chain that I was
   > surprised it hadn't been sued into oblivion."

5. **Sketch-comedy character intros.** Side characters arrive via one sharp,
   funny, slightly cruel physical/behavioral read.
   > "rapidly receding red hair that seemed intent on migrating to his back."
   > "Martha had yet to meet a secret worth keeping."

6. **Playful sound: alliteration & wordplay.**
   > "portly pal," "speeds of my suds"
   Light touch — one flourish per beat, never wall-to-wall.

7. **Melancholy under the jokes.** The humor is a coping layer over real sadness;
   let it show through at the seams.
   > "Like me, the town had lost a part of itself, and, like me, I wasn't so sure
   > it would ever get it back."

8. **Modern, casual reference points.** YouTube shorts, "brain-rot," a WebMD-style
   "online doctor" convincing him he has a brain tumor. Keep it contemporary and
   specific; don't date it with fleeting slang.

9. **Rhetorical questions to the reader / himself.** Marks uncertainty, which is
   the engine.
   > "Were there lighthouses in Redding, Maine?"

## The tonal rule (most important)

The register **shifts with content**, and the prose agent must switch with it:

- **Mundane / social scenes → comedic mode.** Full voice: asides, hyperbole,
  self-deprecation, sketch intros. This is most of a chapter's word count and it's
  where the reader falls in love with Gabriel.
- **Uncanny / horror scenes → restrained mode.** The jokes stop. Sentences get
  cleaner, more sensory, more earnest. Watch it happen in the sample: the overlook
  passage has almost no comedy —
  > "Hand-like waves moved from the shore rather than towards it in a beckoning
  > motion... they made me feel a calm I had never felt before."
  The humor doesn't fight the dread; it *withdraws* and lets the dread land, which
  is scarier because we've been laughing.
- **The return.** After the scare, the comic voice creeps back as a defense
  mechanism ("I decided to walk my insane self back home, reflecting on what an
  idiot I had been") — comedy as the character rationalizing his terror. This
  rhythm — laugh, freeze, nervously laugh again — is the book's heartbeat.

Never crack a joke *inside* the moment of horror. Let him joke before it and after
it, never during.

## Sentence mechanics (as observed)

- Long, winding, clause-stacked sentences for comic riffs and observation;
  short, flat sentences to land a beat or a chill. He alternates the two.
- Contractions throughout; casual register; talks to the reader.
- Concrete, specific nouns do the comic work (a *champagne-scented* candle that
  "smelled like absolutely nothing"), not adjective piles.
- Dialogue is sparse and used for turns — e.g. Rebecca's "Did you go for a walk
  last night or did I dream that?" carries the whole scene's tension.

---

## Diction & register

- Educated but unpretentious; a writer's vocabulary deployed self-mockingly.
- Wry understatement over exclamation. He undersells, he doesn't shout.
- Occasional light profanity for rhythm ("Moving always sucks."), not shock.
- No purple prose. When he reaches for beauty it's in service of melancholy or
  menace, and he keeps it plain.

---

## Do / Don't (for the prose agent)

**Do**
- Open mundane scenes in full comic voice; earn the reader's affection first.
- Foreshadow with one retrospective line before a scene sours.
- Introduce new characters with a single sharp, funny, telling detail.
- Let melancholy show under the humor — failure, aging, self-doubt.
- Drop the comedy and go clean/sensory the instant the uncanny appears.
- Keep the humor as Gabriel's *defense*, especially right after a scare.

**Don't**
- Don't joke during the horror itself.
- Don't run comedy wall-to-wall — the sadness and dread are the point.
- Don't over-alliterate or pun in every line; one flourish per beat.
- Don't go purple or melodramatic; understatement is the house tone.
- Don't use dated slang that will age badly; keep references evergreen-modern.
- Don't let jokes explain the supernatural or puncture its ambiguity.

## Voice fingerprint (quick checklist)

A chapter sounds like *The Strays* when it has:

- [ ] First-person retrospective narration with at least one backward-looking
      foreshadow.
- [ ] Self-deprecating humor rooted in Gabriel's sense of failure.
- [ ] Em-dash / parenthetical asides, including a self-correcting one.
- [ ] One or more sketch-comedy character reads.
- [ ] A clear tonal drop into clean, earnest prose when the uncanny arrives.
- [ ] Melancholy visible under the comedy.
- [ ] Restraint: no joke inside the horror, no purple prose, ambiguity intact.
