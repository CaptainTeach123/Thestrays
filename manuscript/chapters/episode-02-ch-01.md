# Chapter 2: The Crabcake Factory

I had not slept in a month, and I was about to spend an evening convincing a table of strangers that I was fine.

That is not an exaggeration, or not much of one. Somewhere in the small hours I had stopped being a person who occasionally couldn't sleep and become a person who occasionally slept, briefly, by accident, and woke up feeling worse for it. My doctor had warned me that quitting the pills the way I quit them, all at once, down a drain, in a fit of penance, would be "challenging" and "inadvisable." He was right on both counts, which I resented him for.

Rebecca found me in front of the mirror failing to knot a tie. She had been radiant for a month now, since the job, since the house, since the town started being hers in a way it kept refusing to become mine. She took the tie out of my hands and did it herself.

"You look like you haven't slept," she said, kindly.

"New house," I said. "New sounds."

Here is a thing I had gotten good at: telling her a true sentence so I wouldn't have to tell her the real one. The new house did have new sounds. I just wasn't lying awake over the plumbing. I was lying awake over a light on an overlook and a voice in the water and a pair of shoes that had been wet with a fog which, according to every instrument I owned, had never happened. Her hand stayed on my shoulder a second longer than the tie required, and I felt the whole thing climb into my throat, and what I said was, "Are we going to be late?"

We were not going to be late. But the question shut the door I had just cracked open, which was the entire reason I'd asked it.

The Crabcake Factory sat out on the shore, a name so close to a certain national chain that I spent the drive wondering how it had avoided being sued into the sea. Inside it was warm and loud and smelled like butter, and Rebecca was swallowed at once into a knot of people thrilled to see her, which was how I learned that my wife had worked here four weeks and was already beloved. I stood at the edge of it holding a beer I didn't remember being handed and did the arithmetic I always do in those rooms, which is that everyone in them had built something and I had built a reputation for not.

Paul rescued me, or found me, depending on how you scored it. He was a big red-faced man going bald in a direction that suggested the hair was trying to reach his spine, poured into a polo shirt that had long since surrendered, and he carried the easy confidence of a man who has never once lain awake wondering if he was a fraud. I liked him instantly and resented him a little for the same reasons. He was, Rebecca had told me, one of the best neurosurgeons in the state.

"So you're the writer," he said, and shook my hand like he meant it.

"I'm the writer," I agreed, which was the kind of sentence that used to be true.

Someone raised a glass to Rebecca, to new blood, to the surgery center, and the table cheered, and I lifted my beer with everyone else and smiled with everyone else and felt, in the middle of all that warmth, completely alone. It is a particular gift of mine, being lonely in a crowd that has just been kind to me.

Paul, it turned out, was a talker, and drink made him a philosopher. He got onto the brain the way surgeons do, fondly, like a man describing a car he owns. He told a story about a patient who woke up unable to recognize her own husband, and another who smelled burning toast for a week before anyone thought to scan him, and I laughed along and asked, in the light voice I keep for things that are not light, "So how would a person even know? If something up there was going wrong."

"Mostly you don't," Paul said, cheerful as anything. "That's the whole trick of it. The part that's broken is the part you'd use to notice." He tipped his glass back. "The brain is a very good liar. Best one I've ever met."

He meant nothing by it. It was banter, gone from his head before the sentence finished, and I carried it around the rest of the night like a stone in my shoe. Then his pager went off, some emergency that wanted a better man than me, and he left in a squeal of chair legs and apologies, and the one person at that table who might have told me I was well walked out before I could ask him properly.

His seat did not stay empty. Sarah arrived in it the way weather arrives, and her breath announced her a beat before her voice did. She was some distance past her fourth glass and overjoyed to meet me, overjoyed to tell me things, and across the next twenty minutes I received the entire hospital in confidence. Paul, it emerged, was a serial husband and a serial cheater and permanently broke from the arithmetic of both. Jill in administration was something I never fully caught, because Sarah's sentences had begun losing their back halves.

And then, leaning in like she was passing me something valuable: "You have got to meet Craig."

"Craig," I said.

"Craig." She said it as though it answered a question I should have been asking. "Oh, you'll love him. Love-love-love him. One of the good ones, you know?" She wagged a finger in the air between me and the empty chair where Craig apparently stood. "You two. You two are going to get along."

I said something agreeable. But the way she pitched it stuck to me oddly, that you two, like she was introducing me to someone I already knew, and I couldn't have told you why a name I had never heard, hung on a face I had never seen, should leave me feeling I'd forgotten something. I blamed the wine, hers and mine.

That was when I looked past her, out the window, at the water.

The restaurant hung over the shore, and the sea beyond the glass had been an ordinary black all evening. Now there was fog on it. It came up off the water the way it had come up at the overlook, thick and sourceless, gathering where the forecast had sworn there would be nothing, and for the length of one held breath the waves beneath it moved wrong, reaching back into the dark instead of toward the shore. The room drained away. The butter and the noise and Sarah's voice all pulled back like a tide, and my head went quiet, that same terrible quiet, the one that had very nearly walked me off a cliff, except this time I had taken nothing, this time I was sober as a judge and twice as afraid.

Sarah laughed at something, a big wet honk of a laugh, and the room rushed back in. The window showed the ordinary sea. No fog. There is never any fog, afterward. I checked my phone under the table the way a man checks himself for a lump. Clear skies. Of course.

I had spent a month telling myself it was the pills. I did not have the pills anymore.

Rebecca drove us home, because I'd had the beer and, more to the point, because my hands weren't steady and I didn't want her to notice. She was lit up, replaying the night, who was funny and who was a catastrophe and how completely they had all adored me, which was generous of her and of them. I made the right sounds. I watched the black trees slide past and thought about a very good liar living behind my eyes, and about fog that came when I was sober, and I opened my mouth to tell her, finally, the whole of it, the overlook and the water and the shoes and the window, because she is the one person I promised my entire life I would never lie to.

"They loved you," she said, glowing, before I could get a word out. "We really should have Craig over sometime. You'd hit it off."

"Yeah," I said. "We should."

And I shut my mouth around the truth again and agreed to a dinner with a man I had never met and already, for no reason I could put a name to, could not stand the thought of. Outside, the fog stayed off the road the whole way home, which I chose, as I had chosen so much that month, to find reassuring.
