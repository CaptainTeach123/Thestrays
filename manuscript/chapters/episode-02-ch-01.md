# Chapter Two: The Crabcake Factory

**15 July**

A month in. I have not slept properly since we arrived, which my doctor promised would happen when I quit the pills the way I quit them, all at once, down a drain, in a fit of penance. He called it "challenging" and "inadvisable." He was right on both counts, which I resent him for.

I have not told Rebecca about the overlook. A month is a long time to carry a night like that alone, and the carrying is starting to show. She thinks it's the new house, the new sounds. I let her. The new house does have new sounds. I am simply not losing sleep over the plumbing.

Tonight is her work dinner. I have to be charming for a table of people who are better at life than I am. I am writing this instead of getting dressed, which tells you everything.

**16 July, past midnight**

I am writing this down because I need to see it outside my own head, where it has been pacing since ten o'clock.

The dinner itself was fine. Better than fine. Rebecca was swallowed at once into a knot of people thrilled to see her, and I learned that my wife has worked here four weeks and is already beloved. I stood at the edge of it holding a beer I don't remember being handed, doing the arithmetic I always do in those rooms: everyone in them has built something, and I have built a reputation for not.

A surgeon named Paul rescued me, or found me, depending how you score it. Big, red-faced, going bald in a direction that suggests the hair is trying to reach his spine, and carrying the easy confidence of a man who has never once lain awake wondering if he's a fraud. I liked him and resented him for the same reasons. One of the best neurosurgeons in the state, Rebecca says.

He got onto the brain the way surgeons do, fondly, like a man describing a car he owns. A patient who woke unable to recognize her own husband. Another who smelled burning toast for a week before anyone thought to scan him. I laughed along and asked, in the light voice I keep for things that are not light, how a person would even know. If something up there was going wrong.

"Mostly you don't," he said, cheerful as anything. "That's the whole trick of it. The part that's broken is the part you'd use to notice." He tipped his glass back. "The brain is a very good liar. Best one I've ever met."

He meant nothing by it. It was gone from his head before the sentence finished. I have been carrying it around ever since like a stone in my shoe.

Then his pager went off and Paul left in a squeal of chair legs, and a woman named Sarah arrived in his seat the way weather arrives. Some distance past her fourth glass, overjoyed to meet me, overjoyed to tell me things. I received the entire hospital in confidence. Paul is a serial husband and a serial cheater and permanently broke from the arithmetic of both. Jill in administration was something I never quite caught.

And then, leaning in like she was passing me something valuable: "You have got to meet Craig."

"One of the good ones," she said. "You'll love him. Love-love-love him. You two." She wagged a finger between me and the empty chair. "You two are going to get along."

I said something agreeable. But the way she pitched it stuck to me oddly, that you two, like she was introducing me to someone I already knew, and I could not tell you why a name I'd never heard, hung on a face I'd never seen, should leave me feeling I had forgotten something.

That was when I looked past her, out the window, at the water.

The sea beyond the glass had been ordinary black all evening. Now there was fog on it. It came up off the water the way it came up at the overlook, thick and sourceless, gathering where the forecast promised nothing, and for one held breath the waves beneath it moved wrong, reaching back into the dark instead of toward the shore. The room drained away. Sarah's voice, the butter smell, the kitchen clatter, all of it pulled back like a tide, and my head went quiet. That same terrible quiet. Except this time I had taken nothing. This time I was sober as a judge and twice as afraid.

Sarah laughed, a big wet honk of a laugh, and the room rushed back. The window showed the ordinary sea. No fog. There is never any fog, afterward.

I spent a month telling myself it was the pills. I do not have the pills anymore.

Rebecca drove us home, because I'd had the beer and, more to the point, because my hands weren't steady and I didn't want her to see. She was lit up, replaying the night, and I made the right sounds. I watched the black trees slide past and thought about a very good liar living behind my eyes.

I opened my mouth to tell her. All of it. The overlook, the water, the shoes, the window. She is the one person I promised my whole life I would never lie to.

"They loved you," she said, glowing, before I got a word out. "We really should have Craig over sometime. You'd hit it off."

"Yeah," I said. "We should."

And I closed my mouth around the truth again and agreed to a dinner with a man I have never met and already, for no reason I can name, cannot stand the thought of. The fog stayed off the road the whole way home, which I chose, as I have chosen so much this month, to find reassuring.
