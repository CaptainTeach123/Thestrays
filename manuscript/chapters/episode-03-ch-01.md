# Chapter Three: The Good One

**2 August**

I have been dreading a man I have never met for three weeks, which even I can see is not the behavior of a well person.

Rebecca invited Craig the morning after the Crabcake Factory, cheerfully, the way you'd water a plant, and I said yes because saying no would have needed an explanation I don't have. I can't tell her that a name Sarah slurred at me across a table lodged behind my ribs and has been aching there ever since. I can't tell her most things now. So I did what I do with all of it. I cooked.

I am a nervous cook, which makes me a good one. I made too much. I cleaned the house to a standard we do not actually live at. Lela watched me from the doorway with the particular skepticism she has aimed at me since the overlook, since the night she hauled me off a cliff and I repaid her by pretending it never happened.

**2 August, late**

I have to get this down exactly, while I still half-believe it.

I opened the door and I was standing on the other side of it.

I want to be careful, because I have gone over this more times than is healthy and the words keep failing it. It was not that Craig looked like me, the way people look like each other, a shared nose, a similar build. It was my face. My exact face, the one I shave, arranged into a warm easy smile I have never once managed, over a nicer coat than I own. For one long airless second the floor felt very far from me.

"You must be Gabriel," my mouth said, from his head. "I've heard a criminal amount about you."

And Rebecca came around me and hugged him, this man, my face, and said something about the wine, and did not scream, and did not stagger, and did not so much as blink. She hung his coat. She was happy. Whatever I was seeing, I was seeing alone, and some cold clerk at the back of my mind noted that and filed it, because a man who sees things no one else can see has a name for what's wrong with him, and it is not a ghost.

I would have survived the face. The face I might have argued myself out of. It was the rest of him that took me apart.

He sat where I would have sat. He talked with his hands the way I talk with mine, that same chopping gesture I've caught myself doing in videos and hated. When Rebecca praised his work he ducked his head and waved it off with the exact shrug I use to wave off praise, the one that means don't, I'll only disappoint you later, and I felt it move in my own shoulder like a phantom limb. He told a story about getting lost on the drive over and landed it on a dry little joke against himself, timed the way I time mine, in words I could have sworn I had used, and Rebecca laughed the way she laughs at me on my good days.

I sat at my own table and hosted my own manner and said almost nothing. Anything I might have offered, he had already said, a half-beat sooner and better, as though reading from a script I hadn't known I'd written. I kept hunting his face for the seam, the tell, the place where the mask stopped and a stranger began. There wasn't one. The longer I looked the deeper it ran, past the face, into the way he took up the room, and I understood I could look all night and only ever find myself.

"You alright?" he asked me, kindly. Even the kindness sat exactly where I keep mine.

"Long week," I said, which was the first true thing I'd managed all evening.

Rebecca caught my eye with a look I have learned to read in eleven years of marriage. Be normal. You're embarrassing me, a little. And underneath, the thing she'd never say in company, that she was worried, and had been for a while, and was getting tired of pretending with me that she wasn't.

He left a little after ten. At the door he shook my hand, and his grip was my grip, and he held on half a second past where a man lets go of someone he just met. "We should do this again," he said, warm as anything. "Feels like I already know you."

Then he was gone down the dark drive, and Lela, who had spent the whole night pressed flat to the far wall watching him, made a single low sound I have never heard her make and would be glad never to hear again.

I told myself I wanted proof. I wanted to hold the thing up to the light and see it plainly for whatever it was. The hospital keeps a staff page. I found him between a nurse and an anesthesiologist.

I sat on the edge of the bathtub with the screen going bright and dim in my hand and I could not make the photograph and the evening fit inside the same head. Either the thing that ate at my table tonight wore a face no camera can hold, or I spent an entire dinner watching my own face bloom across a man who does not have it, hearing my own voice come out of a mouth that isn't mine.

I have been sitting here a long while trying to decide which of those I could live with. I am still trying.

I lay down beside her and tried to call up Craig's face, to hold it still and study it, the round pleasant stranger from the website. I couldn't. Every time I reached for it, it came back mine.

I could not assemble a single sentence, not one, that would explain to her that the good one who came to dinner had, for one whole evening, been me.

Outside, past the trees, the sea went on doing whatever the sea does at night. I chose not to listen to it. I am getting good at choosing not to listen.
