# Chapter 3: The Good One

I had been dreading a man I'd never met for three weeks, which even I could see was not the behavior of a well person.

Rebecca had invited Craig to dinner the morning after the Crabcake Factory, cheerfully and without a second thought, the way you'd water a plant, and I had said yes because saying no would have required an explanation I didn't have. I couldn't tell her that a name Sarah had slurred at me across a table had lodged somewhere behind my ribs and been aching there ever since. I couldn't tell her most things, lately. So I did what I did with all of it. I cooked.

I am a nervous cook, which means I am a good one. I made too much. I cleaned the house to a standard we do not actually live at. Over a cutting board I rehearsed the way a rested and honest man would feel about company, pleased and uncomplicated and normal. Lela watched me from the doorway with the particular skepticism she'd been aiming at me since the overlook, since the night she hauled me off a cliff and I repaid her by pretending it never happened.

"You're doing the thing," Rebecca said, coming through in earrings, smelling like the good perfume. "The one where your shoulders are up around your ears."

"This is just where my shoulders live now," I said.

She laughed and kissed me and told me I'd love Craig, everyone loved Craig, and for a moment, with her hand flat on my chest, I almost told her. Not about Craig. About all of it, the light and the water and the fog that came when I was sober. The truth was right there, sitting up in my throat the way it did. Then the doorbell rang and the moment closed the way a door closes, and I went to answer it.

I opened the door and I was standing on the other side of it.

I want to be careful here, because I have gone over this more times than is healthy and the words keep failing it. It was not that Craig looked like me, the way people look like each other, a shared nose, a similar build. It was my face. My exact face, the one I shave, arranged into a warm easy smile I have never once managed, over a nicer coat than I own. For one long airless second the floor felt very far from me.

"You must be Gabriel," my mouth said, from his head. "I've heard a criminal amount about you."

And Rebecca came around me and hugged him, this man, my face, and said something about the wine he'd brought, and did not scream, and did not stagger, and did not so much as blink. She hung his coat. She was happy. Whatever I was seeing, I was seeing alone, and some cold clerk at the back of my mind noted that and filed it, because a man who sees things no one else can see has a name for what's wrong with him, and it is not a ghost.

I would have survived the face, I think. The face I might have argued myself out of. It was the rest of him that took me apart.

He sat where I would have sat. He talked with his hands the way I talk with mine, that same chopping gesture I've caught myself doing in videos and hated. When Rebecca praised his work at the hospital he ducked his head and waved it off with the exact shrug I use to wave off praise, the one that means don't, I'll only disappoint you later, and I felt it move in my own shoulder like a phantom limb. He told a story about getting lost on the drive over and landed it on a dry little joke against himself, timed the way I time mine, in words I could have sworn I'd used, and Rebecca laughed the way she laughs at me on my good days.

I sat at my own table and hosted my own manner and said almost nothing. What was there to say. Anything I might have offered he had already said, a half-beat sooner and better, as though he were reading from a script I hadn't known I'd written. Once he caught me staring and glanced back, friendly, checking I was still with him, and I made myself look at my plate. I had been hunting his face for the seam, the tell, the place where the mask stopped and a stranger started. There wasn't one. The longer I looked the deeper it ran, past the face, into the way he took up the room, and I understood I could look all night and only ever find myself.

"You alright?" he asked me, kindly. Even that. Even the kindness sat exactly where I keep mine.

"Long week," I said, which was the first true thing I'd managed all evening.

Rebecca caught my eye across the table with a look I have learned to read in eleven years of marriage. It said be normal. It said you are embarrassing me, a little. And underneath, the thing she would never say in front of company, it said that she was worried, and had been for a while, and was getting tired of pretending with me that she wasn't.

He left a little after ten. At the door he shook my hand, and his grip was my grip, and he held on half a second past where a man lets go of someone he only just met. "We should do this again," he said, warm as anything. "Feels like I already know you."

Then he was gone down the dark drive, and Lela, who had spent the whole night pressed flat to the far wall watching him, made a single low sound I had never heard her make and would be glad never to hear again.

"That went well," Rebecca said, collecting glasses. "See? He's lovely."

"He's lovely," I agreed, and went to find my phone.

I don't know what I expected. Proof, I suppose. I wanted to hold the thing up to the light and see it plainly for whatever it was. The hospital keeps a staff page, headshots in tidy rows, and I found him there between a nurse and an anesthesiologist. Craig. Same first name, same department, the man Rebecca had hugged in my hallway an hour before.

He looked nothing like me.

Nothing. Not a little, not a cousin's worth, not the generous resemblance you might grant a stranger in bad light. A round, pleasant face I had never seen in my life. Brown eyes where mine are gray. A good ten years between us. I sat on the edge of the bathtub with the screen brightening and dimming in my hand and I could not make the photograph and the evening fit inside the same head. Either the thing that ate at my table tonight wore a face no camera could hold, or I had spent an entire dinner watching my own face bloom across a man who does not have it, hearing my own voice come out of a mouth that isn't mine.

I sat there a long while trying to decide which of those I could live with. I am still trying.

Rebecca was already in bed when I came up, warm and loose and glad.

"I'm so happy you two got on," she said into the pillow. "I told you. One of the good ones."

"Yeah," I said. "He was great."

I lay on my back and stared at the ceiling and tried to call up Craig's face, to hold it still and study it, the round pleasant stranger from the website. I couldn't. Every time I reached for it, it came back mine. I lay there in the dark beside the sleeping woman I had promised my whole life never to lie to, and I could not assemble a single sentence, not one, that would explain to her that the good one who came to dinner had, for one entire evening, been me.

Outside, past the trees, the sea went on doing whatever the sea does at night. I chose not to listen to it. I was getting good at choosing not to listen.
