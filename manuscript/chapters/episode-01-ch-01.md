# Chapter 1: The Move

The fog came up off the water like a held breath let go, and somewhere inside it I decided, calmly and completely, that I was going to walk off the edge of the world.

I want to be clear that it did not feel like despair. It felt like relief. It felt like the first quiet my head had offered me in forty years, and all I had to do to keep it was take three steps toward the sound rising out of the water: a voice, almost, folded into the waves, saying something I couldn't understand and didn't need to. And the waves reached for me. Not the way waves reach. They curled back from the shore, palm-up, beckoning, and I leaned toward them the way you lean toward sleep.

That was our first night in the new house.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is the one instinct I've never had to work at. Let me back up.

Moving always sucks. At its core it's a pessimistic act, a public, sometimes too public, admission that you'll trade the good of one place for the theoretical better of another. We had gotten good at it. Four homes, three states, and roughly a thousand boxes had made us, if nothing else, experienced. This move was supposed to be different. We were "setting down roots," a phrase we'd started using with a straight face, and which managed the neat trick of making us feel settled and elderly at the same time.

The roots were Rebecca's. My wife had been offered her dream job running a hospital's surgery center, and since I hadn't worked much since finishing my last novel (if a thing still qualifies as a novel when no one reads it), I played the good soldier, packed the boxes, and off we went to start over. I had once been, per the Times, a "promising writer with unlimited potential." Several failed projects later, I had excelled at exactly one thing: proving my early supporters wrong.

The drive from Eugene to Redding, Maine went shockingly well. Even our dog, Lela, behaved, which I chose to read as an omen. It was one, just not the kind I had in mind.

Redding had seen better days. The listings had sold me a town that hadn't existed in some time; what we drove into was a mostly vacant downtown wearing the last good decade like a coat two sizes too big. Rebecca did her best to talk up the newer parts near the shore, where a handful of businesses had survived by existing solely to serve outsiders. We ate outstanding seafood. We overpaid, shamefully, bordering on exploitatively, for a champagne-scented candle that, to me at least, smelled like absolutely nothing. Like me, I caught myself thinking, the town had lost a part of itself and wasn't sure it would ever get it back. I tried not to think it. I thought it the rest of the day.

We collected the keys from Martha, our real estate agent, who in the time it takes a normal person to say hello had briefed me on her divorce, her sciatica, and a skin condition I would have paid actual money not to know about. Then we saw the house, and I forgave the town a little. Near the water, private, ringed with trails: essentially our dream home. Standing in the empty rooms before the movers arrived, it already felt like one.

Our first night did not go the way I'd hoped. Rebecca fell asleep like a woman with a clear conscience and an excellent job. I stayed up, unpacked a few boxes, watched five hundred YouTube shorts I was too old to understand, and then, in the part of the night I would give anything to redo, took a sleeping pill strong enough to drop a horse. Then, because I am not a smart man, I clipped on Lela's leash and took her out for a walk I do not remember choosing to take.

The night was almost perfect, a soft damp breeze off the water taking the edge off the summer heat. Lela trotted ahead, happy, down a trail that climbed toward a stony overlook. Somewhere on the way up, the pill found me, and I understood in a dim underwater way that I had made a mistake. That was when the fog rolled in.

It shouldn't have. It came up thick off the water while the trail ahead stayed clear, and above me, where the overlook should have been, there was a light I didn't remember being there. Or that had never been there. Were there lighthouses in Redding? I didn't know. On a night already built entirely out of bad decisions, I climbed toward it.

Lela wasn't bothered by any of it, which is the detail that still keeps me up. If it was real, why wasn't she afraid? And if it wasn't, why did it feel more real than my own hands?

At the top I stood inside a ring of light the fog would not enter. I walked to the edge and looked down, and that is where you came in: the water moving wrong, reaching, the voice threaded through the waves, the terrible tranquility of it. For the first time in my adult life my mind went quiet. I was not a failure. I was not anything at all. And I decided, the way you decide to sit down when you're tired, that I would step off into the calm.

Then Lela lost her mind.

She barked like I had never heard her bark, threw her whole weight backward, and the leash cinched my wrist so hard I was sure she had broken it. She hauled me off that edge, physically, bodily, off it, and the instant my heels found solid ground the whole thing came apart, and I saw the size of what I had almost done. I sat in the dirt and wept and held my dog and asked her out loud what was wrong with me.

When I stood up, there was no light. No fog. Only an ordinary dark and an ordinary sea. I checked the weather on my phone the way you'd check a fever. Clear, it said. It had never mentioned fog at all.

I walked my insane self home, poured the sleeping pills down the drain, and swore I would never touch them again. It was so easy to blame the medication. It was easier than the alternative, which I was not ready to hold yet: that maybe the pills weren't the problem, and something else was.

The next morning Rebecca was up first, making coffee, humming a song I couldn't name, so relaxed and so happy in our new kitchen that I could not bring myself to set any of it at her feet. She looked up, smiled, and asked, lightly, "Did you go for a walk last night, or did I dream that?"

I have spent my whole marriage trying to be honest with this woman.

"No," I said. "Just walked the property with Lela. Finished a couple of boxes."

It is the easiest lie I have ever told, and the one I would give anything to unsay. Because when I went to put my shoes away that morning, they were still wet, soaked through and gritted with sand, and I had not gone anywhere near the water.

Or I didn't remember going. By then I was already learning that in Redding, those are not the same thing. And that the distance between them was going to cost me everything I loved.
