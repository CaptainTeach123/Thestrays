# Chapter One: The Move

*[The journal opens with a single loose sheet, undated, in a hurried hand. It has been placed first. — Ed.]*

The fog came up off the water like a held breath let go, and somewhere inside it I decided, calmly and completely, that I was going to walk off the edge of the world.

It did not feel like despair. It felt like relief. I am writing that down before I lose the nerve, because if I say it out loud to anyone with a medical degree they will take my shoelaces.

**16 June**

Rebecca thinks I should be writing. She is always right, so here I am, writing. Not the novel. The novel and I are no longer on speaking terms. This.

Moving always sucks. At its core it is a pessimistic act, a public, sometimes too public, admission that you'll trade the good of one place for the theoretical better of another. We have gotten good at it. Four homes, three states, roughly a thousand boxes. This one was meant to be different. We are "setting down roots," a phrase we started using with a straight face, and which manages the neat trick of making us feel settled and elderly at the same time.

The roots are Rebecca's. My wife has landed her dream job running a hospital's surgery center, and since I haven't worked much since finishing my last novel (if a thing still qualifies as a novel when no one reads it), I played the good soldier, packed the boxes, and off we went. I was once, per the Times, a "promising writer with unlimited potential." Several failed projects later, I have excelled at exactly one thing: proving my early supporters wrong.

The drive from Eugene to Redding, Maine went shockingly well. Even Lela behaved, which I chose to read as an omen. It was one. Just not the kind I had in mind.

Redding has seen better days. The listings sold me a town that hadn't existed in some time; what we drove into was a mostly vacant downtown wearing the last good decade like a coat two sizes too big. We ate outstanding seafood down by the shore, where the businesses that survive do it by serving outsiders. We overpaid, shamefully, for a champagne-scented candle that smells to me like absolutely nothing. Like me, I caught myself thinking, the town has lost a part of itself and isn't sure it will get it back. I tried not to think it. I thought it the rest of the day.

Martha talks the way weather happens. In the time it takes a normal person to say hello, she briefed me on her divorce, her sciatica, and a skin condition I would have paid real money not to know about. But the house forgave the town for me. Near the water, private, ringed with trails. Standing in the empty rooms before the movers came, it already felt like ours.

**16 June, later. Very late.**

I have done something stupid and I need it on paper while it's still bright, before morning sands it down into a dream.

Rebecca fell asleep like a woman with a clear conscience and an excellent job. I stayed up, unpacked, watched five hundred YouTube shorts I am too old to understand, and took a sleeping pill strong enough to drop a horse. Then, because I am not a smart man, I clipped on Lela's leash and took her out for a walk I don't remember deciding to take.

The night was almost perfect. Lela trotted ahead, happy, up a trail toward a stony overlook. Somewhere on the climb the pill found me and I understood, in a dim underwater way, that I had made a mistake. That was when the fog rolled in.

It shouldn't have. It came up thick off the water while the trail ahead stayed clear, and above me, where the overlook should have been, there was a light I didn't remember being there. Or that had never been there. I climbed toward it, because on a night already built out of bad decisions, why not one more.

Lela wasn't bothered by any of it, which is the part I can't set down. If it was real, why wasn't she afraid? And if it wasn't, why did it feel more real than my own hands?

At the top I stood in a ring of light the fog would not enter, and I looked over the edge, and the water was moving wrong. The waves curled back from the shore, palm-up, beckoning, and there was a sound in them, a voice almost, saying something I couldn't understand and didn't need to. My head went quiet. For the first time in my adult life I was not a failure, I was not anything, and I decided, the way you decide to sit down when you are tired, that I would step off into the calm.

Then Lela lost her mind. She barked like I have never heard her bark and threw her whole weight backward, and the leash cut into my wrist, and she hauled me off that edge. The instant my heels found solid ground the whole thing tore open and I saw the size of what I had almost done. I sat in the dirt and wept and held my dog.

When I stood, there was no light. No fog. Ordinary dark, ordinary sea. I checked the weather the way you check a fever.

**17 June**

Rebecca was up first, making coffee, humming a song I can't name, so happy in the new kitchen that I couldn't lay any of it at her feet. She looked up, smiled, and asked, light as anything, "Did you go for a walk last night, or did I dream that?"

I have spent my whole marriage trying to be honest with this woman.

"No," I told her. "Just walked the property with Lela. Finished a couple of boxes."

It is the easiest lie I have ever told, and the one I would give anything to unsay. Because when I went to put my shoes away this morning they were soaked through, and the cuffs of my jeans with them, damp to the shin. The ground was dry. The app still swears the night was clear. The only thing I walked through that could have left me like that was the fog. The fog I have spent all morning deciding I imagined.

I am telling this journal a great many things I cannot tell her. I am already learning how much easier the lie is than the truth, and that the distance between the two is going to threaten everything I love.
